# Urban Lifestyle Commercial — V2 Revision Brief

Revise the current commercial to improve spatial realism, natural human behavior, facial stability, visual continuity, and audio continuity. Preserve the core story, approved protagonist, wardrobe, and English-language brand identity.

The goal is a coherent, believable commercial rather than a sequence of individually generated clips. Correct structural and performance problems before applying sharpening, upscaling, or final color grading.

Timestamps below refer approximately to the current 59-second V1 edit. Recheck cut positions against the editing timeline before making changes.

## 1. Subway Geometry — Around 3 Seconds

The relationship between the doors, carriage layout, and apparent direction of travel looks incorrect. The passenger boarding doors should be located on the sides of the carriage, parallel to its longitudinal direction of travel.

Establish a physically coherent subway interior:

- Clearly define the carriage's longitudinal axis.
- Place passenger boarding doors on the left and right side walls.
- Keep any end-of-car gangway door visually distinct from side boarding doors.
- Align windows, seats, poles, ceiling rails, and the aisle with the same structure.
- Make exterior motion through the windows consistent with the train's direction of travel.
- Keep this geometry stable across the wide shot and close-up.

Correct the storyboard or first frame before regenerating motion. Do not try to compensate for an incorrect layout with camera movement.

## 2. Subway Passengers — Entire Opening Scene

The passengers currently look like a static background. Give them subtle, independent, motivated behavior.

- Standing passengers shift their weight gently with the carriage movement.
- A passenger holding a pole makes small balance adjustments through the shoulders, elbow, and wrist.
- People looking at phones make occasional small thumb movements and natural eye shifts.
- The people sharing a phone briefly exchange glances, speak quietly in turns, and react with restrained smiles.
- Include occasional natural blinks and small changes in posture.
- Stagger individual actions; do not make the entire carriage sway or blink in synchronization.
- Keep movement subtle enough to preserve the protagonist as the visual focus.

Use a continuous low-level bed of rail rumble, ventilation, and indistinct passenger conversation. Give the shared-phone pair a little quiet conversational presence without intelligible competing dialogue. A soft noise bed may support the ambience, but it should sound like a subway, not generic white noise.

## 3. Emotional Performance — Around 5–7 Seconds

During “I just don't know how to start,” the protagonist should show a small amount of sadness, hesitation, and vulnerability. A neutral or smiling expression does not communicate this moment clearly enough.

Use a restrained sequence:

1. He looks briefly at the nearby people interacting comfortably.
2. His gaze pauses, suggesting that he would like to join.
3. He lowers or withdraws his gaze slightly.
4. His mouth relaxes and the corners soften very subtly.
5. He releases a small breath and returns his attention to the window.

Keep the emotion understated: a fleeting sense of disappointment rather than despair, panic, or melodrama. This emotional direction takes precedence over an entirely neutral expression.

The line remains off-screen narration. His lips should not form speech sounds or move in sync with the narrator. If necessary, generate the visual performance without narration and add the voiceover during editing.

## 4. Eye Misalignment and Facial Drift — Around 8 Seconds

The eye on the protagonist's right side appears visibly misaligned or unstable around this point. Review both eyes frame by frame to identify the full affected interval.

- Keep eye positions, pupil placement, eyelid shape, and facial proportions anatomically stable.
- Both eyes must look toward the same plausible target.
- Prevent an eye from sliding across the face as the head moves.
- Preserve identity during blinks and changes in angle.
- Check the face immediately before and after the cut, not only in the first frame.

If the defect persists across multiple frames, regenerate or replace the affected shot. Sharpening and color correction cannot repair unstable anatomy.

Background people also need review here. They may be softly defocused, but their heads, faces, and bodies should retain believable shapes and coherent motion.

## 5. Global Eye Performance — Throughout the Video

The protagonist's eyes often appear stiff or unfocused. Give each shot a specific gaze target and a purposeful sequence of eye movements.

Examples:

- Subway: nearby passengers' faces, then his reflection in the window.
- Office: the departing coworker's face, then the laptop, then the phone.
- Refrigerator: the product display, then the specific can he reaches for.
- Riverside: the water or distant buildings, then the can or phone.
- Home: the phone screen and a small reaction to what he reads.

Use natural fixation, brief eye movements between targets, occasional blinks, and small head adjustments. Match convergence to target distance. Avoid vacant staring, wandering pupils, repeated mechanical blinking, and accidental eye contact with the camera.

Do not rely on the instruction “natural eyes” alone. Specify what he looks at, when his attention changes, and what motivates the change.

## 6. Global Human Detail — Throughout the Video

The protagonist and background people often look soft, blurry, or unnatural in both their faces and bodies.

- Keep the main subject sufficiently resolved for the shot size.
- Preserve natural skin texture without waxy smoothing or artificial edge enhancement.
- Keep facial structure stable during motion.
- Maintain coherent shoulders, neck, arms, hands, clothing, and body proportions.
- Ensure background people remain physically plausible even when out of focus.
- Avoid smeared faces, melting silhouettes, fused limbs, and drifting clothing edges.
- Keep movement readable instead of hiding unstable anatomy behind excessive blur.

Separate intentional depth of field from generation defects. A defocused person can look soft while still having a stable silhouette and believable motion. Higher export resolution alone does not restore missing or unstable source detail.

Inspect source images and generated clips before upscaling. Use an approved identity reference consistently and regenerate defective shots selectively.

## 7. Office-to-Store Transition — Around 19–20 Seconds

The scene change and music change feel abrupt.

### Visual revision

Connect departure and arrival through a clear action sequence: leaving the office corridor, approaching or opening the store door, then entering. Preserve a compatible direction of movement and avoid cutting between unrelated walking poses.

### Audio revision

Keep one continuous music track across the transition. Introduce a little store ambience before the picture changes, then let the office or corridor ambience recede. Synchronize the entrance chime with the door action.

Do not restart the music or switch abruptly to a different musical phrase simply because a new generated clip begins.

## 8. Product-to-Riverside Transition — Around 32–33 Seconds

The visual and sound transition into the riverside sequence feels too sudden.

### Visual revision

Let the product close-up reach a clear endpoint, then establish the riverside with a wide shot before moving into a closer performance shot. Give the viewer enough time to understand the new location.

Use a deliberate cut at a musical phrase or a matching visual action. Do not apply a dissolve automatically if the two compositions do not support it.

### Audio revision

Begin quiet river and distant-city ambience slightly before the location cut. Carry the music across the transition without interruption. Fade out refrigerator or shop ambience naturally rather than dropping every sound at once.

## 9. Audio Break — Around 42 Seconds

The audio does not connect smoothly through the phone sequence.

Maintain the same riverside ambience across the wide shot, phone insert, and facial reaction. A change in framing should not reset the acoustic environment.

Check for abrupt silence, clipped sound tails, music restarts, changes in noise floor, and sudden loudness differences. Use short, carefully placed audio overlaps or crossfades where needed. Place any phone tap or notification sound on the matching action.

## 10. Phone Interface — Around 43 Seconds

The phone screen is not readable: text becomes gibberish and profile images look mosaic-like or corrupted.

Create the interface as a separate, clean graphic and composite it onto the screen.

- Use a stable blue-and-white layout.
- Keep the title **SUPER DRY CLUB** legible.
- Use clean, consistent profile images.
- Keep text short, correctly spelled, and entirely in English.
- Track the interface to the phone's perspective and movement.
- Preserve realistic reflections, screen brightness, and finger occlusion.
- Match screen sharpness and motion blur to the surrounding footage.

Give the interface a clear narrative purpose. For example, show a simple invitation and a **Join** button, followed by one deliberate tap and the protagonist's reaction. If using a reply such as **See you there**, establish the invitation first.

Do not ask the video model to regenerate exact interface typography and avatars independently in every frame.

## 11. Disconnected Scene Change — Around 45 Seconds

The visual and sound change feels disconnected from the preceding riverside sequence.

Remove the return-to-convenience-store phone shot unless it has a clearly established narrative purpose. Use a simpler progression:

**Riverside phone interaction → protagonist's reaction → protagonist replying at home → woman receiving or reading the response.**

Carry the music through the sequence. Introduce home room tone gradually when the location changes. Do not use a visual transition to conceal an unexplained change in time or place.

## 12. Male-to-Female Transition — Around 53 Seconds

The picture and sound change between the two characters feels abrupt.

Build a clear cause-and-effect relationship:

1. The protagonist completes a message or taps send.
2. Cut to the woman receiving or noticing the message.
3. She reads and reacts naturally.

Use matching gestures, screen direction, or eyelines to support the cut. A single restrained notification sound can bridge the action. Keep the music continuous and avoid giving each character a separate musical start.

## 13. Transition to Product Hero — Around 56 Seconds

The cut to the final product shot feels disconnected visually and sonically.

Allow the character's reaction to finish, then cut to the product at a clear musical transition or resolution. Relate the product composition to the established silver-blue color palette.

Let the music continue and resolve over the final product frame. Avoid a sudden switch to unrelated product-shot music or an unnecessarily dramatic impact sound. Hold the ending long enough for the brand to register.

## 14. Additional Continuity Corrections

### Office objects and action order

The laptop closes and then appears open again in the following phone shot. Correct the order to **check phone → close laptop → leave**, or use a tighter phone shot that does not reveal a contradictory laptop state. Keep the cup, desk objects, and their positions consistent.

### Product packaging

Keep one approved English-language package across the refrigerator, hand-held close-up, riverside, and final hero shot. Check the main brand, red label text, capacity marking, proportions, and blue/silver layout. Fix unstable small text through compositing where necessary.

### Performance variation

Do not end every action with a smile. Use different emotional beats: hesitation on the subway, a polite nod at work, quiet recognition at the refrigerator, physical relaxation at the river, and a more noticeable smile when connection occurs.

Keep the protagonist's attention on the people and objects within the scene rather than on the camera. During the refrigerator pickup, let him lower the can naturally instead of presenting it toward the audience.

## 15. Audio Assembly Requirements

Build the final soundtrack as a continuous edit:

- One coherent background-music track or deliberately arranged musical sequence.
- One consistent narrator voice and delivery.
- Separate environmental beds for subway, office, store, riverside, and home.
- Individually timed effects for doors, footsteps, refrigerator movement, can handling, and phone interaction.
- Controlled overlaps and fades between acoustic environments.

Useful local effects from generated clips may be retained. Do not concatenate each clip's complete music-and-ambience mix without checking continuity. Remove or replace conflicting music layers.

Check narration intelligibility, loudness consistency, sound tails, and every identified transition on headphones and speakers.

## 16. Revision Priorities

### Priority 1 — Spatial and human credibility

Correct subway geometry, eye misalignment, unfocused gaze, frozen passengers, and unstable faces or bodies. Regenerate only the shots that cannot be repaired cleanly.

### Priority 2 — Editing and sound continuity

Repair the transitions around 19–20, 32–33, 42, 45, 53, and 56 seconds. Remove the unnecessary store return. Rebuild the soundtrack so it continues across clip boundaries.

### Priority 3 — Screen and product accuracy

Replace the corrupted phone interface and stabilize product packaging. Confirm that the phone interaction communicates a meaningful step toward connection.

### Priority 4 — Final image finishing

After structural fixes, match exposure and color between shots. Reduce excessive sharpening, harsh facial highlights, or overly strong blue/gold saturation where they make the footage look artificial. Keep the phone's light on the face faint and diffused rather than theatrical.
